Chapter 1 · The evolution of the idea

Beyond Static EMRs: The Emergence of Contextual Clinical Intelligence

From diagnosis-aware documentation to composable care pathways, patient monitoring, trajectory analytics and a connected clinical knowledge ecosystem.

The starting point is a practical frustration: a clinical record can be full of data and still fail to capture the information that matters to the clinical problem in front of the clinician. The five essays that seed this chapter describe an architectural progression; later chapters build on it. Each step addresses a limitation exposed by the previous one.

1. The problem with static records

Traditional electronic medical records are often organized around encounters, generic documentation templates and retrospective record keeping. They can store diagnoses, observations, medications and notes, but they do not necessarily adapt the structure of documentation to the clinical situation. A patient with acute snake envenomation may be presented with much the same generic form as a patient attending for a minor musculoskeletal complaint.

This is not merely a user-interface inconvenience. The structure of the record influences what is remembered, prompted, captured and later available for analysis. In a time-sensitive presentation, clinically important details may be buried in narrative text or omitted because the form does not ask for them explicitly.

Illustrative example — snake envenomation

The interval between the bite and the first administration of anti-snake venom (ASV), relevant clotting-test results, dose and administration details, and adverse reactions may all matter to the clinical record and subsequent cohort analysis. If these are not represented as identifiable, timestamped data, later analysis must attempt to reconstruct them from notes.

The larger issue is that the data model is often designed independently of the clinical questions that clinicians, researchers and quality-improvement teams may want to ask. Analysts then spend substantial effort locating, reconciling and interpreting information before they can investigate the medicine itself.

The first architectural shift is therefore to make the clinical situation influence the data structure at the point of care.

2. Diagnosis as an active schema modifier

The Context-Aware EMR Enhancer introduces a simple principle: the diagnosis should help determine which clinical information the system asks the clinician to capture. Rather than requiring clinicians to search through generic menus or remember every disease-specific variable, a diagnosis can initiate the generation or retrieval of a focused clinical context.

2.1 From diagnosis to structured clinical fields

A clinician enters a diagnosis, potentially with additional instructions or an institutional protocol context. An LLM-assisted workflow can draft a set of disease-relevant fields. Those fields then pass through deterministic validation and clinical review before being used. The intended output is not just a paragraph of generated text: it is a structured representation that can be rendered in the EMR and mapped to appropriate interoperability resources.

2.2 Interoperability is part of the design

The proposed architecture uses HL7 FHIR R4 resources as a representation and exchange foundation, with SNOMED CT and LOINC bindings where appropriate. The intention is to avoid creating a proprietary data silo: a generated field should have a defined clinical meaning, a data type, a provenance trail and an appropriate mapping. Terminology bindings must be verified rather than accepted simply because a language model produced them.

2.3 The first analytical payoff

Once clinically meaningful fields are captured consistently, the system can begin to examine timing, completeness, protocol-related process measures and terminology-binding coverage across cases. For example, a cohort may be examined for the distribution of bite-to-treatment intervals or the proportion of cases with documented clotting-test results. These are analytical opportunities, not proof that the data are complete or that any particular treatment caused an outcome.

The first essay therefore establishes the foundation: clinical context should shape data capture, and structured capture should make subsequent analysis less dependent on retrospective text mining.

3. From one diagnosis to composable contexts and pathways

A diagnosis-driven form is a useful first step, but real patients rarely fit one diagnosis at a time. A patient admitted with diabetic ketoacidosis may also have chronic kidney disease, an infected foot ulcer, hypertension and a need for glucose monitoring. A single disease template cannot adequately represent all these dimensions.

The next step is to move from one diagnosis-specific form to a composable case model. The case begins with a narrative and can be enriched as the clinical picture evolves. Instead of choosing one monolithic template, the clinician applies independent element sets that can coexist.

3.1 The element-set model

KindMeaningExample
diseaseA disease-specific clinical contextSnake envenomation; DKA
careA care or monitoring context not defined by a diagnosisHbA1c monitoring; glucose monitoring
templateA general-purpose documentation structureBasic history, examination and plan
pathwayAn ordered bundle of other element setsDKA admission pathway
customA clinician- or institution-defined setUnit-specific checklist

In the proposed case model, the narrative remains the root. Applied element sets are recorded independently, along with their identifiers and application times; entered values are associated with the relevant elements. The case can therefore expand without losing the provenance of which modules were applied and when.

3.2 Disease context is not the same as care context

A disease context helps describe what condition is being considered. A care context describes how the patient is being assessed, treated or monitored. HbA1c monitoring or a glucose-monitoring protocol can be relevant across multiple diagnoses. Treating these as reusable care elements avoids embedding the same monitoring logic separately inside every disease template.

3.3 Pathways as reusable compositions

Clinicians often want a useful starting structure for common scenarios. A pathway can be a named, ordered bundle of element sets applied together. A DKA admission pathway might include vital-sign capture, a basic EMR template, the DKA disease context, glucose monitoring and other locally approved monitoring elements.

A pathway is a starting point, not a lock-in. After applying it, the clinician can remove unnecessary sets or add others. The system should retain the source pathway and version so that pathway use and subsequent modifications can be analyzed. Pathways should be clinician- or institution-defined, reviewed and versioned; they should not be generated as authoritative clinical protocols by an LLM.

3.4 Care has a timeline

A conventional form often captures a single value. Clinical care involves repeated measurements and events. Fields that support repetition can store timestamped observations, and the interface can display them as trajectories. This is the transition from a static form to a record that can represent change over time.

Architectural principle: Start with the patient's narrative, compose the relevant disease and care contexts, and preserve repeated observations as a longitudinal trajectory.

4. Contextual recommendation and patient monitoring

Once a library of element sets exists, another problem appears: clinicians should not have to browse a large catalogue and know in advance which modules to apply. A passive module marketplace simply moves the burden from a long form to a long list.

The Contextual Module Recommender is intended to inspect the active encounter and surface a small number of relevant modules. The design uses a hybrid approach:

  1. Index approved modules. Each module carries structured applicability metadata, such as relevant clinical concepts, observations, encounter context and defined eligibility criteria.
  2. Trigger from the active case. The recommender receives relevant patient and encounter information through internal events or standards-based integration such as CDS Hooks.
  3. Apply deterministic eligibility rules. Terminology hierarchies, value sets and explicit thresholds identify which modules are eligible.
  4. Use AI for ranking and explanation. An LLM may help prioritize eligible candidates and explain their relevance, but it does not decide eligibility alone.
  5. Suppress unnecessary repetition. Recent documentation is checked so that valid, recently completed assessments are not repeatedly requested.
  6. Record feedback. Accepted, dismissed and modified recommendations are logged and reviewed to improve the module catalogue.

4.1 Example: multimorbidity

Consider a patient with type 2 diabetes, CKD stage 3b and a foot ulcer. The system might recommend a diabetic-foot assessment and a renal medication review, while suppressing a screening module that has already been completed within its valid interval. These are illustrative module names and triggers; actual eligibility criteria and clinical thresholds require clinician and informatician definition and validation.

4.2 The same module can serve clinicians and patients

A module can have a clinician-facing projection inside the EMR and a patient-facing projection for home monitoring. The clinical concepts and extraction rules remain shared, while the wording and interaction adapt to the audience. A clinician may document orthopnoea and peripheral oedema; a patient-facing check-in can ask whether they needed extra pillows or noticed more ankle swelling.

Patient-reported observations can return to the longitudinal record, where predefined escalation workflows may route concerning responses to the clinical team. Thresholds must be explicitly approved for the relevant condition and setting. A generic consumer health tracker is not the goal: the design is for short, focused, condition-specific check-ins connected to the patient's care plan.

The emerging loop: catalogue → recommend → compose → patient check-in → structured extraction → improved recommendations. The loop is a design direction that requires testing; it is not an autonomous clinical decision-maker.

5. Analytics designed into the record

The next essay in the progression asks what happens when context-aware capture, composable element sets and longitudinal observations are designed with analytics in mind from the beginning.

Traditional analytics often begins after documentation is complete. Analysts identify a question, extract records, reconcile terminology, search narrative text, reconstruct timelines and create a study-specific dataset. Much of the effort is spent preparing data rather than investigating clinical processes.

A context-aware architecture can reduce repeated reconstruction by capturing clinically meaningful variables when they are known, representing module and pathway membership explicitly, and preserving timestamped observations.

5.1 Disease-specific and multimorbidity analytics

Structured disease contexts can support cohort characterization, clinical completeness analysis, protocol timing and subgroup selection. Composable contexts extend this to multimorbidity. Diabetes, CKD, foot-ulcer and infection contexts can be examined individually and in combination, rather than forcing researchers to reconstruct their intersection from fragmented notes.

5.2 Pathways become measurable objects

If pathway membership and version are explicit, institutions can examine pathway utilization, missing components, process milestones, deviations and differences between pathway versions. An important distinction must be preserved: applying a pathway does not prove that its clinical recommendations were followed. Adherence requires evidence from the relevant actions, observations and events.

5.3 Longitudinal and time-to-event analytics

Repeated observations support trend analysis, rates of change, first and last values, time windows and trajectory comparisons. Timestamped events can support questions such as time from presentation to diagnosis, diagnosis to intervention, time to documented improvement, time to complication and time to discharge.

When treatment events and observations share a timeline, analysts can investigate what happened before and after an intervention. These analyses are descriptive or exploratory unless supported by an appropriate study design. Temporal order and association do not establish treatment effectiveness or causality.

5.4 Analytics across the hospital and beyond

The same data model can support patient-level monitoring, disease-level cohorts, pathway evaluation, institutional quality improvement and research. Patient-generated data can extend the timeline after discharge, making follow-up participation and post-discharge trajectories more visible.

Analytics domainQuestions made easier to investigate
Disease-specificWhat are the clinical characteristics, severity parameters and documentation gaps?
MultimorbidityHow do combinations of conditions relate to different care patterns and trajectories?
Protocol and pathwayWere process steps documented, when did they occur, and where were deviations observed?

Every measure still needs an explicit population, denominator, time window, missing-data policy and validation plan. Better data architecture makes analysis more accessible; it does not make every analytical conclusion automatically valid.

6. Beyond an intelligent form: the wider clinical intelligence vision

The fifth essay points toward a broader destination: an EMR should not merely display a diagnosis-specific form or trigger a recommendation. It should participate in a larger architecture that connects patient context, clinical knowledge, reasoning, evidence, workflows, monitoring and learning.

This distinction matters. Interoperability of data does not automatically produce interoperability of clinical intelligence. A system may exchange FHIR resources while failing to understand the clinical trajectory represented by their relationships. A decision-support alert may detect one condition without understanding the wider care context. A population analytics platform may identify a pattern without connecting it to the clinical reasoning and processes that produced it.

6.1 The knowledge-to-practice lifecycle

The broader architecture links several activities that are often separated:

  • Clinical concepts and relationships define the meaning of information.
  • Patient-specific context determines which knowledge is relevant.
  • Clinical reasoning interprets observations, uncertainty and alternatives.
  • Dynamic documentation captures the information needed for the case.
  • Decision support presents explainable, context-relevant suggestions.
  • Clinicians make and document decisions.
  • Longitudinal monitoring observes what happens afterward.
  • Population analytics and research identify patterns and gaps.
  • Quality improvement evaluates whether knowledge or processes should change.
UnderstandClinical concepts, patient context, evidence and uncertainty
↓
ComposeClinical contexts, element sets and care pathways
↓
Recommend and actExplainable support with clinician authority
↓
Monitor and learnLongitudinal observations, outcomes, analytics and evaluation
↓
ImproveReviewed updates to knowledge, workflows and analytical definitions

This is a knowledge flywheel, not an unrestricted self-learning clinical system. Observed associations do not automatically establish causality. AI-generated changes to clinical knowledge should not become authoritative merely because a model proposed them. Clinical governance, review, versioning, provenance and auditability are foundational.

6.2 Preserve uncertainty and provenance

A diagnosis may be provisional and later revised. A laboratory value is a measurement; an interpretation of its significance is a different kind of assertion. A guideline recommendation is not identical to a mandatory action. A population-level estimate is not automatically applicable to an individual patient.

The architecture must therefore distinguish documented facts, clinician interpretations, derived values, model-inferred suggestions and approved decisions. It should also distinguish clinical certainty from computational confidence, and association from causation.

6.3 The human and organizational dimension

Technical feasibility is not enough. A system must fit clinical work, remain maintainable, be acceptable to its users and be evaluated in the environment where it is deployed. Recommendation overload, governance bottlenecks, conflicting modules, inconsistent terminology and alert fatigue can undermine an otherwise elegant design. Implementation and evaluation belong inside the architecture, not at the end of it.

The Contextual Clinical Intelligence Architecture

From passive medical records to knowledge-driven, trajectory-aware and continuously evolving clinical intelligence.

This brings together several complementary ideas: context-aware electronic medical records, composable clinical pathways, clinical analytics, the Avinash Principle and its concept of key decision nodes, clinician-classified patient phenotypes, historical patient journeys, and evidence-based clinical recommendations.

The clinical record becomes an active source of clinical intelligence rather than a passive repository of documentation.

The objective is not simply to create another EMR, clinical decision support system or analytics platform. It is to create an integrated environment in which the EMR understands the evolving clinical situation, recognizes relevant patterns, identifies important decision points and makes appropriate medical knowledge available at the right moment.

2. The Narrative History Box Becomes the Intelligence Entry Point

One of the most important architectural ideas is that the first element of a clinical case does not necessarily need to be a diagnosis or a disease-specific form. It can simply be the patient's narrative.

The narrative can originate from:

  • A patient describing symptoms in their own language.
  • A patient completing a conversational history.
  • A clinician entering the history.
  • A patient-clinician conversation transcribed into text.
  • Imported information from a referral or previous medical record.

The system interprets the narrative and constructs a provisional understanding of the clinical situation.

Example initial narrative

"A 58-year-old diabetic patient presents with vomiting, reduced urine output and generalized weakness for two days. The patient has a history of hypertension and previously elevated creatinine."

Identified context Intelligence opportunity
Diabetes Diabetes-related clinical context
Reduced urine output Renal assessment and AKI decision nodes
Vomiting Dehydration and metabolic assessment
Previous elevated creatinine Baseline renal function comparison
Hypertension Relevant comorbidity context

These are provisional interpretations, not automatically confirmed diagnoses. The clinician can accept, modify or reject them.

The system need not wait for a diagnosis before becoming intelligent.

This extends the original context-aware EMR concept: clinical intelligence can begin from the first narrative rather than waiting for the completion of structured documentation.

3. Avinash Principle: The Decision Intelligence Layer

In this book, the Avinash Principle is the idea that clinical reasoning pivots on a small number of recurring key decision nodes (hub nodes) in a patient's journey, and that these nodes can be captured as reusable, knowledge-backed units and then tested for recurrence across patients.

A disease context tells the EMR what information is relevant to a condition. A pathway tells it how clinical care can be organized. A decision node tells it where clinical reasoning requires attention and what important decision needs to be considered at that point in the patient's journey.

Illustrative decision-node sequence

01. Recognize the clinical problem Diabetes, vomiting and oliguria. Is there evidence of acute deterioration?
↓
02. Establish renal baseline What was the previous creatinine? Is current renal function different?
↓
03. Evaluate possible mechanisms Is there evidence of volume depletion, intrinsic renal injury or obstruction?
↓
04. Identify important investigations Which assessments and investigations can distinguish the possibilities?
↓
05. Review treatment implications Are medications, fluid management and monitoring appropriate?
↓
06. Track the trajectory Is renal function improving, deteriorating or remaining unchanged?

Rather than displaying an entire textbook or a massive checklist, the system identifies relevant decision nodes and makes them visible when appropriate.

A decision node could contain:

  • The clinical question.
  • Why the question matters.
  • What information is required.
  • Which evidence or guideline supports it.
  • Which possible clinical branches follow.
  • Which observations or events should be monitored.
  • What uncertainty remains.
  • Which external application or calculator can help.

A decision node is not simply a reminder. It is a reusable, knowledge-backed unit of clinical reasoning.

4. Historical Patient Phenotype Intelligence

This is another particularly powerful extension. Traditional patient similarity searches often begin with diagnosis codes, age, sex, laboratory values or comorbidities.

This architecture goes further: it attempts to identify patients who resemble the current patient in their clinical phenotype and trajectory, not merely their diagnostic labels.

Example: Clinician-classified snake envenomation phenotypes

Imagine a clinician-researcher studying 200 patients with snake envenomation and classifying their clinical journeys into meaningful phenotypes.

Possible phenotypes

  • Early systemic envenomation.
  • Delayed presentation with coagulopathy.
  • Neurotoxic presentation with evolving neurological manifestations.
  • Significant adverse reactions to treatment.

Trajectory patterns

  • Deterioration despite initial treatment.
  • Unexpected recovery or complications.
  • Unusual clinical progression.
  • Journeys influenced by socioeconomic or behavioral factors.

These classifications need not be mutually exclusive. A patient may belong to multiple phenotypic groups.

What happens when a new patient enters?

  1. Construct a provisional patient phenotype from available information.
  2. Compare it with clinician-defined phenotype definitions.
  3. Retrieve relevant historical patient groups.
  4. Compare their clinical trajectories.
  5. Identify shared and divergent decision nodes.
  6. Show what happened to similar patients at important transition points.

Instead of merely identifying patients with the same diagnosis, identify patients who share meaningful clinical patterns and trajectories.

Connecting phenotype intelligence with Avinash Principle

The two ideas reinforce one another:

  • Individual clinical reasoning generates candidate hub nodes.
  • Population-level trajectory analysis examines whether those nodes recur across multiple patients.
  • Repeated patterns can identify important decision points within a phenotype.
  • Trajectory divergence can reveal nodes associated with different outcomes.

This connects clinical reasoning, clinical phenotyping, longitudinal analytics and knowledge discovery.

6. The Narrative as an Independent Clinical Intelligence Source

The narrative is not merely a convenient way to collect data before generating structured forms. It is an independent source of clinical information.

Consider these statements:

  • The patient cannot afford to remain admitted.
  • The family intends to take the patient home.
  • The patient has previously discontinued treatment.
  • The patient travels several hours to reach the hospital.
  • The patient has difficulty understanding the treatment plan.
  • The patient is reluctant to disclose certain symptoms.
  • The patient has responsibilities that make regular follow-up difficult.

A conventional EMR might place these observations in social history or miscellaneous notes. A contextual intelligence system can recognize that some of these observations may materially alter the patient's clinical trajectory and eventual outcome.

A truly intelligent EMR needs both physiological understanding and an understanding of the patient's lived circumstances.

The system should not confuse a patient's circumstances with a biological diagnosis, but it should not ignore those circumstances when understanding the clinical trajectory.

7. Existing Clinical Applications Become Part of the Intelligence Ecosystem

You do not have to rebuild every useful medical application. The EMR can become the contextual orchestrator of existing tools.

It could connect to:

  • Clinical risk calculators.
  • Drug interaction checkers.
  • Renal dose references.
  • Guideline applications.
  • ECG interpretation systems.
  • Imaging decision-support applications.
  • Clinical literature search engines.
  • Laboratory trend visualization tools.
  • Remote patient monitoring applications.

Existing tools retain their functionality and validation. The EMR provides the clinical context, orchestration and a unified view of their outputs.

For example, a clinician opens a patient with diabetes, CKD and an evolving acute illness. Rather than manually navigating unrelated applications, the EMR surfaces the relevant tools alongside the appropriate decision nodes.

This is an ecosystem approach, not an attempt to build one monolithic medical application.

A Clinical Knowledge and Intelligence Infrastructure

Three connected ideas for moving beyond static EMRs: a standard for clinical contexts and pathways, compositional contextual analytics, and a reusable decision-node analytics engine.

These three ideas are potentially the foundation of a much bigger architecture: a standardized, composable, context-driven clinical intelligence system. They should not be treated as three independent features. They are complementary architectural layers.

1. Clinical Context Standard

A standardized way to represent clinical context, pathways, decision nodes and their relationships—not merely how clinical data is entered.

2. Contextual Analytics Templates

Reusable, extensible analytical definitions covering individual diseases, comorbidities and eventually the breadth of clinical medicine.

3. Decision-Node Analytics Engine

An execution engine that analyzes clinically meaningful nodes: symptoms, signs, history, tests, diagnoses, medications, events and outcomes.

Core architectural principle: Clinical data, clinical context, clinical pathways and analytical logic should be separate but interoperable.

1. An openEHR-like standard, but for clinical context rather than just data entry

The foundational idea

Existing standards address important but different questions. openEHR provides clinical information models, archetypes and templates. HL7 FHIR supports interoperable exchange and representation of healthcare information. SNOMED CT provides clinical concepts and relationships, while LOINC standardizes observations and measurements.

Your proposed standard would address a complementary question:

How should clinical knowledge be represented as reusable, composable, executable and analytically meaningful clinical contexts?

1.1 Distinguish the clinical objects

  • Diabetes as a clinical concept — a coded concept.
  • Diabetes as a disease context — a domain of relevant information and knowledge.
  • Diabetes monitoring as a care context — a set of monitoring needs and activities.
  • Diabetes management as a pathway — an ordered or conditional clinical workflow.
  • HbA1c as a clinical decision node — a meaningful observation used in assessment and decisions.
  • HbA1c monitoring as an analytical definition — a reusable way to examine measurements over time.
  • Diabetes with CKD as a compositional context — a combined context that brings relevant definitions together.

These objects are related, but they are not interchangeable.

4.1 Analytics generated from the same knowledge objects

Do not make analytics templates a completely separate, manually maintained library. Let the clinical knowledge standard describe the analytical capabilities of each object.

5. An additional idea: analytics should be generated from the same knowledge objects

This is a further architectural refinement: analytics definitions should be linked to the clinical objects they analyze, rather than maintained as disconnected dashboard code.

For example, a clinical element definition could state that a measurement is numeric, repeatable, timestamped and bound to a terminology concept. Its analytics specification could declare that distribution, trend, threshold and time-window operators are applicable. A context can then reuse it in multiple analytical views.

This makes the analytics library more maintainable: when a clinical definition changes, its dependent analytical definitions can be identified, validated and versioned.

7. Important architectural safeguards

  1. Clinical assertion versus inference: A clinician-recorded finding must remain distinguishable from an AI-inferred finding.
  2. Versioning: A change in a clinical context or pathway must not silently alter the interpretation of historical cases.
  3. Terminology validation: Generated SNOMED CT and LOINC mappings must be verified, not trusted merely because an LLM produced them.
  4. Analytics reproducibility: Every result should preserve its definition, version, population, time window and execution parameters.
  5. Missingness: Not documented must never automatically mean absent or negative.
  6. Clinical safety: Analytics and observational associations must not silently become treatment recommendations.
  7. Composability: Shared elements should not be duplicated when several diseases or pathways use them.

These safeguards are essential if the system eventually becomes a shared standard rather than simply an application-specific architecture.

7. Engineering and clinical safety principles for every future task

  1. Preserve the core product boundary. Documentation and trajectory layer first; integrate adjacent hospital systems only when justified.
  2. Keep the internal model authoritative. FHIR R4 is the exchange/export boundary; SDC import/export maps to the same internal model.
  3. Never edit applied migrations. Add numbered migrations and test both clean installs and upgrades from prior versions.
  4. Do not hard-delete clinical data. Use archive, entered-in-error or amendment workflows with reasons and audit history.
  5. Pin versions. A case must retain the exact element-set and pathway versions applied to it. Updates are explicit, never silent.
  6. Derive authorship from the session. Signer, author and approver must not be trusted from typed fields or request bodies.
  7. Enforce permissions on the server. Every new route must be included in the route/permission matrix and audited where it changes state.
  8. Keep AI bounded. AI may draft, extract candidates, rank or explain. It does not create authoritative doses, thresholds, protocol bands or approval decisions.
  9. Keep AI off by default. Provider keys remain server-side. Preview, de-identification, explicit send, rate limits and audit behavior remain testable.
  10. Preserve uncertainty and provenance. Do not turn an inference into a verified fact or an association into causation.
  11. Use synthetic data during development. No real patient data until the relevant governance, security, validation and deployment gates are satisfied.
  12. Build a test with every behavior. Small changes should add focused tests and keep the existing suite green.

1. Why a canonical model is necessary

The product is at risk of becoming a set of adjacent features if each capability invents its own representation of the patient. A registration screen has a patient row; a case screen has a local object; a pathway has a list of fields; a flowsheet has values; an AI request has a prompt payload; and an analytics view has a separate cohort structure. If these structures do not share stable identifiers and explicit relationships, the application cannot reliably explain which observation belongs to which patient journey, which definition generated a field, which version was applied, or which evidence supported a recommendation.

The Clinical Context Model is the semantic contract shared by the user interface, API, PostgreSQL, clinical knowledge and analytics. It does not mean that every feature must use one giant JSON object or one enormous table. It means that each bounded entity has a clear identity, ownership, relationship and lifecycle.

Without a canonical model

  • Duplicate patient and case representations.
  • Values detached from their definitions or units.
  • Pathways that cannot be reproduced after an update.
  • AI output mistaken for verified clinical data.
  • Analytics that cannot explain their source population.

With a canonical model

  • Stable patient, encounter and case identity.
  • Observations linked to definitions and provenance.
  • Applied content pinned to exact versions.
  • Explicit review before suggestions become facts.
  • Reproducible analysis over traceable data.

6. Observation semantics and longitudinal time

Repeated observations are central to the product's trajectory layer. A value is not fully described by a number alone. It needs a meaning, a time, a unit, a source and a status. The system should distinguish at least two times:

  • Effective time: when the observation was measured, reported or clinically true.
  • Recorded time: when it was entered or received by the application.

This distinction matters for imported laboratory results, retrospective documentation, home measurements and delayed data entry. A trend should normally be ordered by effective time, while an audit view can show when the system learned about the value.

6.1 A measurement is not just a scalar

{
  "fieldKey": "serum_creatinine",
  "value": { "type": "quantity", "number": 1.4 },
  "unit": { "system": "http://unitsofmeasure.org", "code": "mg/dL", "display": "mg/dL" },
  "effectiveAt": "2026-11-22T08:30:00+05:45",
  "recordedAt": "2026-11-22T08:42:00+05:45",
  "source": { "type": "imported", "system": "synthetic-lab", "reference": "lab-result-42" },
  "status": "final"
}

This example illustrates structure only; it is synthetic and does not establish a clinical threshold or interpretation. Units must be explicit where applicable. Conversion must be deliberate and tested. A derived value must record its formula/version and references to the source observations. Missing, not measured, not applicable, unknown and entered-in-error are different states and must not all be represented as an empty string or zero.

6.2 Repeating observations should be append-oriented

Do not overwrite a prior measurement when a new measurement arrives. Add a new row. If a value was entered incorrectly, preserve the original and create a correction/amendment or entered-in-error status with an audit trail. This protects longitudinal interpretation and supports reproducible analytics.

7. Versioning, provenance and audit invariants

7.1 Three independent version dimensions

Version dimensionWhat changesWhat must remain stable
Database schema versionTables, columns, constraints and indexes through numbered migrations.Already-applied migration files and their checksums.
Knowledge-definition versionElement sets, pathways, decision nodes, phenotype and analytics definitions.Previously published versions and case references to them.
Clinical record revisionCase state, notes, observations and corrections.Original authored/signed content and audit history.

Do not use one generic version field to mean all three. Each has a different owner, lifecycle and compatibility rule.

7.2 Provenance is a first-class property

Every clinical fact should answer: who or what supplied it; when it was clinically true; when it was recorded; whether it was verified; which source or definition it came from; and whether it has been corrected. A practical source classification includes patient-reported, clinician-entered/verified, imported, device-generated, derived and AI-proposed. Do not collapse provenance, certainty and model confidence into one number.

7.3 Non-negotiable invariants

  • Patient, case, encounter, note, observation and audit records are not hard-deleted.
  • Signed notes are immutable; corrections are amendments with reasons.
  • Case applications pin exact element-set and pathway versions.
  • Updating a library definition never changes an existing case without an explicit action.
  • Author, signer, reviewer and approver are derived from the authenticated session.
  • Server-side authorization applies to every read and write; hiding a button is not security.
  • AI output is marked as proposed and requires human review before becoming verified data.
  • Unverified terminology codes are not exported as valid FHIR Coding entries.
  • Every clinically meaningful write is audited, preferably in the same database transaction.
  • Analytics runs record the definition version and enough input metadata to reproduce the method.

11. Acceptance tests and the first vertical slice

11.1 Data integrity

  • A case cannot reference a nonexistent patient.
  • A case can link to more than one encounter without duplicating the case.
  • An applied element set points to an immutable version.
  • A repeated observation keeps its original effective and recorded timestamps.
  • Invalid values, unknown field keys and invalid units are rejected or explicitly represented as unknown—not silently coerced.

11.2 Authorization, audit and lifecycle

  • Every new route is covered by the permission matrix for all roles.
  • Author and actor are session-derived; forged body fields are ignored/rejected.
  • Clinical write and audit row commit or roll back together.
  • Clinical tables cannot be hard-deleted or truncated by the app role.
  • Signed-note amendments preserve the signed original and require a reason.
  • Two concurrent edits produce a clear conflict rather than silent data loss.

11.3 Clinical semantics and longitudinal correctness

  • Patient-reported, clinician-verified, imported, derived and AI-proposed values remain distinguishable.
  • Trend views order by effective time and can reveal recorded time.
  • Units and terminology codes are preserved and validated.
  • Updating a published library version does not alter historical case composition.
  • Export mapping does not invent unsupported FHIR semantics or emit unverified codes.

11.4 Recommended first demonstration

Use a synthetic patient with a diabetes-and-CKD example solely to demonstrate architecture, not to encode treatment guidance. Create one persistent case, apply a general template and two composable contexts, enter a small sequence of timestamped synthetic observations, display the timeline, and verify that the case survives restart and a second-browser session. Confirm audit entries, version pins and verified-only coding behavior.

The first slice should not include AI diagnosis, treatment recommendations, protocol thresholds, predictive phenotype matching, real-patient deployment or marketplace distribution. It should prove that the core data model works before adding intelligence on top.

Chapter 5 conclusion

The Clinical Context Model is not a new screen and not a universal JSON container. It is a set of explicit entities and lifecycle rules that preserve the relationship between a person, a care interaction, an evolving clinical problem, the content applied to that problem, and the facts recorded over time. Its value is that every later capability—pathways, decision nodes, evidence, trajectories, analytics and AI—can operate on the same traceable foundation.

The implementation strategy is intentionally conservative: preserve the existing Node.js/PostgreSQL architecture, finish the current safety work, map the prototype before migrating it, and add persistence in small verified slices. Build the data model so that clinical meaning and provenance survive every transformation. Intelligence can then be layered onto something reliable.

4. Compatibility: the key marketplace capability

The marketplace becomes meaningfully useful when choosing a context automatically narrows the analytics catalogue to packages that can actually operate on that context. This is a formal compatibility problem, not just a search or recommendation feature.

4.1 Three levels of compatibility

Context compatibilityDoes the analytic declare support for this contextual element/pathway and its version?Compatible, incompatible or needs an approved adapter
Schema compatibilityDo required concepts, value types, units, terminology and time semantics match?Requirements satisfied, partial or missing

4.2 Compatibility matching sequence

  1. Read the selected context and pathway IDs, versions and their declared dependencies.
  2. Retrieve analytics metadata from the local catalogue index.
  3. Filter by declared context/pathway compatibility and supported schema versions.
  4. Resolve required concepts and terminology bindings against the imported data dictionary.
  5. Check data types, units, cardinality, time windows, status and provenance requirements.
  6. Evaluate whether required data are present and valid in this specific case.
  7. Return a transparent compatibility explanation and any missing requirements.

5.2 A reusable operator library

Do not implement every analytic as a bespoke script. Build a small, explicit set of reusable operators that packages can compose. Candidate operators include:

  • Concept and value extraction, filtering and cohort inclusion/exclusion.
  • Presence, completeness, missingness and documentation coverage.
  • First, last, minimum, maximum, mean, median and count over defined windows.
  • Time differences between explicitly defined clinical events.
  • Rate of change, trend segmentation and trajectory summaries.
  • Threshold counts and duration-above/below-threshold, only when the threshold definition and units are explicit.
  • Pathway step presence, ordering, timing and documented deviation.
  • Cross-context joins, subgrouping and stratification.
  • Descriptive distributions and validated score calculations.

Each operator needs a typed input/output contract, deterministic behavior, unit tests, boundary tests and explicit missing-data behavior. Clinical scores or thresholds must use reviewed definitions and validated implementations; an LLM must not invent formulas at runtime.

5.3 Analytics package versus engine operator

An operator is a generic computational capability, such as calculating a time interval or summarizing a trajectory. An analytics package is a clinically meaningful composition of operators, input requirements, parameters, outputs and interpretation notes. For example, a “renal function trajectory” package may request a defined renal measurement, normalize compatible units, select a time window, calculate descriptive change and render a trajectory. The package declares the clinical question; the engine supplies the reusable computation.

5.4 Deterministic core, AI-assisted edges

Use deterministic code for compatibility, calculations, cohort membership, data transformations and result generation. AI may assist with narrative interpretation, package discovery, natural-language explanations or authoring drafts, but AI output must be labelled, reviewable and kept separate from computed facts. An LLM must not silently alter data, eligibility rules, thresholds or analytical formulas.

6. Import, normalization and patient-context assembly

The import pipeline is the trust boundary between the Enhancer export and the analytics runtime. A successful file parse is not enough: the app must establish what the data mean, which versions produced them and whether they satisfy the analytics contracts.

6.1 Import pipeline

  1. Identify package format and declared export/schema version.
  2. Validate file structure, required fields and integrity.
  3. Resolve patient/case/encounter references without exposing unnecessary identifiers.
  4. Load applied element and pathway IDs with exact versions.
  5. Normalize observations while preserving original value, unit, effective time, status and source.
  6. Resolve terminology only through explicit, verified mappings.
  7. Preserve narrative text and distinguish it from extracted or inferred concepts.
  8. Produce an import report showing accepted, rejected, unmapped and ambiguous records.

6.2 FHIR R4 and SDC support

FHIR should be supported as an interoperability boundary, not assumed to be the app's internal database schema. The import layer should support the Enhancer's documented native export first, then add FHIR R4 Bundle import using explicit resource mappings. Contextual element definitions may be represented as FHIR Questionnaire resources, with SDC extensions and behavior where supported. Completed answers may arrive as QuestionnaireResponse, while observations and clinical events may be represented through their appropriate FHIR resources.

A Questionnaire is not the entire patient case model. The adapter must reconstruct relationships among Patient, Encounter, Condition, Observation, Questionnaire, QuestionnaireResponse, CarePlan and Provenance as appropriate to the defined semantics. Do not force a one-to-one mapping where none exists. Support a declared FHIR version/profile and report unsupported resources rather than silently discarding them.

6.3 Preserve temporal and epistemic meaning

Every observation should retain value, unit, effective time, recorded time where available, status, source, author/performer where available, and verification/provenance. The engine must distinguish absent data from a negative finding, an unknown value, a not-applicable field and a measurement that was never collected. It must also distinguish patient-reported facts, clinician-confirmed facts, imported facts and machine-generated suggestions.

7. Running many analytics and presenting results

The user should be able to select multiple compatible analytics and run them as one analysis batch. The planner can reuse imported data and shared intermediate computations, but every analytic retains its own input contract, version, parameters, status and result record.

7.2 Result types

  • Single-case results: completeness, trends, time intervals, decision-node status and pathway process summaries.
  • Longitudinal results: repeated measurements, trajectories, changes over time and event timelines.
  • Population results: cohorts, distributions, subgroup comparisons and pathway summaries, only when the dataset and governance permit them.
  • Combined-context results: analyses requiring more than one selected context, with the contributing contexts and dependencies shown.

Each result should display its clinical question, population or case scope, exact package version, data period, input coverage, exclusions, missing-data policy, denominator where relevant, units, calculation timestamp, engine version and limitations. A chart without its definition and provenance is not a sufficient clinical analytic result.

7.3 Results are not recommendations by default

A descriptive analytic result should not automatically become a diagnosis, treatment recommendation or alert. If a package is intended to support a clinical decision, that intended use, evidence basis, validation scope and governance pathway must be explicit. The interface should preserve the distinction between measured data, calculated results, AI-generated explanations and clinician interpretation.

8.1 Validation is scoped, not a universal badge

A package may be technically validated for schema conformance and deterministic execution but not clinically validated for patient care. A clinical validation claim must identify the intended population, care setting, reference standard or comparator, dataset, sample and evaluation method, performance results, known limitations, reviewer qualifications and date. Validation for one population or FHIR profile does not automatically transfer to another.

8.2 Review workflow

  1. Author creates a package from a schema-validated template.
  2. Automated checks run: schema, terminology references, dependencies, tests, security and compatibility fixtures.
  3. Author submits an immutable version snapshot.
  4. Independent reviewers assess clinical meaning, evidence, safety, usability and intended scope.
  5. Validation evidence is attached and status is assigned by an authorized governance process.
  6. Release is tagged and indexed; subsequent changes require a new version and repeat the required checks.
  7. Post-release issues can trigger restriction, deprecation or withdrawal without erasing historical provenance.

9. Privacy, safety and execution boundaries

  • Patient data stay out of GitHub. The marketplace contains definitions, code, documentation and synthetic test fixtures only. Never publish identifiable patient records, private exports or real clinical examples without an appropriate lawful and ethical basis.
  • Pin and verify packages. Fetch a specific version or immutable release, validate its manifest and verify integrity before use. Do not execute arbitrary code directly from a moving branch.
  • Constrain execution. Prefer a declarative analytics DSL and a fixed operator library. If executable extensions are later permitted, isolate them with strict resource, filesystem and network restrictions and an explicit review process.
  • Keep calculations reproducible. Record the exact input snapshot or a privacy-preserving reference, package version, engine version, parameters and execution environment.
  • Make data quality visible. Never silently coerce incompatible units, guess terminology mappings or treat missing values as normal values.
  • Separate discovery from clinical authority. Marketplace ranking and package status must not be represented as proof that a package is appropriate for an individual patient.
  • Use synthetic data first. Real-patient use requires appropriate consent/legal basis, access controls, security review, privacy assessment, clinical validation, workflow evaluation and regulatory assessment where applicable.

The first product can be a local-first application that processes an imported dataset on the user's device, with optional controlled persistence. This reduces unnecessary transfer of patient data, but local execution still needs secure handling, clear retention controls and protection against malicious packages.

Chapter 7 · The shared contract behind the ecosystem

The Clinical Context Intelligence Standard: A Unified Architecture for Authoring, Interoperability and Execution

A native standard for contextual elements, pathways and analytics—with equivalent LLM, GUI and code authoring, plus a FHIR interoperability mode.

The application architecture and marketplace become genuinely composable only when every published artefact follows a common contract. A contextual element created in the EMR Enhancer, a pathway assembled from several elements, and an analytic authored for those elements must describe themselves in a predictable way. The marketplace must be able to compare their meaning and requirements; reviewers must be able to inspect them consistently; and the runtime must be able to interpret them without bespoke integration for every package. This chapter proposes the formal standard that supplies that contract.

Central design principle

One canonical clinical-intelligence model; multiple authoring experiences and exchange formats; one conformance pipeline; one marketplace contract; one runtime representation.

1. Why the architecture needs a formal standard

The architecture described in the preceding chapters defines the major components: contextual elements, pathways, analytics, marketplace, compatibility resolver and execution engine. But component boundaries alone do not guarantee interoperability. If two authors represent the same clinical concept using different field names, data types, timing rules, terminology bindings or dependency declarations, a marketplace cannot reliably determine whether an analytic is applicable.

The standard must make the important properties machine-readable and reviewable: what an artefact is; what clinical meaning it carries; which data it expects or creates; how it relates to other artefacts; what it depends on; how it is versioned; what evidence supports it; what it is allowed to do at runtime; and what tests or reviews it has passed.

  • For authors: a predictable target for LLM, GUI and code generation.
  • For the marketplace: searchable metadata and computable compatibility.
  • For reviewers: stable sections and comparable evidence.
  • For the engine: deterministic inputs, outputs, dependencies and execution semantics.
  • For users: transparent reasons why an analytic is available, conditional or incompatible.

The standard is not simply a form schema. It is a family of related specifications for clinical meaning, workflow composition, analytics, interoperability, governance and execution.

2. What to learn from openEHR, FHIR and FHIR SDC

The project should learn from established health-information standards rather than invent replacements for problems they already solve. At the same time, it should avoid assuming that any one of them covers the complete product architecture.

Similarly, openEHR archetypes and templates can inform clinical modelling and may be mapped or exchanged where appropriate, but the project should not claim openEHR conformance merely because it borrows its modelling ideas.

Practical rule: reuse external standards for the domains they define well; define explicit adapters and mappings for the remaining application-specific concepts.

3. The proposed native standard and its boundaries

The native standard should be a versioned specification suite, not one enormous schema. It should define a canonical semantic model and a set of interoperable artefact contracts. The initial suite can be called the Clinical Context Intelligence Standard (CCIS) as a working name; naming and namespace governance can be finalized before public release.

3.1 Specification layers

  1. Core envelope: identity, type, canonical version, publisher, lifecycle, dependencies, provenance and licensing.
  2. Clinical content: concepts, data elements, value constraints, units, terminology, timing and epistemic status.
  3. Composition: grouping, pathway stages, transitions, conditional inclusion and dependencies.
  4. Analytics: input contracts, operators, parameters, output contracts, interpretation and execution profile.
  5. Interoperability: FHIR profiles/SDC mappings, terminology mappings and optional openEHR mappings.
  6. Governance and assurance: review records, validation scope, test evidence, safety classification and lifecycle.

3.2 Separate the specification from its serializations

The canonical model defines meaning. JSON/YAML documents, FHIR resources, GUI forms and code are ways to author or serialize that meaning. The runtime should consume a normalized, versioned intermediate representation generated by a conformance pipeline. It should not infer clinical semantics from arbitrary UI layout or from an LLM's prose.

For example, a creatinine observation should declare its clinical concept, numeric type, permitted unit(s), reference or interpretation policy if applicable, effective time, specimen or method context when relevant, source and missingness semantics. Do not force every element to contain every possible property. The specification should define required core properties and conditional modules, with explicit extension points. Extensions must be namespaced, documented and testable; unknown mandatory extensions must cause a safe compatibility failure rather than being silently ignored.

5. Pathway and composition specification

A pathway is a versioned clinical workflow and knowledge composition. It can reference contextual elements and groups, define stages and decision points, and express conditional transitions. It should not duplicate the full definitions of the elements it uses.

5.1 Pathway contract

  • Stable identity, version, scope, intended use and exclusions.
  • Referenced contextual elements and compatible version ranges.
  • Stages, entry criteria, exit criteria and optional/required status.
  • Decision nodes with explicit conditions and outcomes.
  • Transitions and branching rules with defined evaluation semantics.
  • Timing constraints, follow-up windows and event triggers where appropriate.
  • Required and optional analytics, clearly distinguished from clinical actions.
  • Evidence references, review scope, test cases and provenance.

Pathways should describe a workflow and the information needed to support it. They should not silently authorize treatment or automate high-impact clinical actions. Any recommendation or action logic needs its own clinical governance, human oversight and deployment controls.

5.2 Composition rules

Composition must define conflict handling when two elements request overlapping information, how shared concepts are identified, how version constraints are resolved, and whether a dependency is mandatory or optional. The user interface may combine repeated fields where their semantics are genuinely equivalent, but must not merge merely similar labels.

6. Analytics package specification

An analytics package is a reproducible, inspectable computation over a declared clinical data contract. It is not just a script or a dashboard. Its specification must allow the marketplace to decide whether it can run and allow the engine to run it safely and consistently.

6.2 Separate analytic definition from reusable operators

Operators are small, reviewed computational building blocks—such as date arithmetic, unit conversion, cohort filtering, rate calculation or trajectory summarization. Analytics compose operators into a defined computation. This permits shared testing and consistent semantics without turning every analytic into arbitrary executable code.

Prefer a declarative operator graph for the common case. Where code is necessary, use a constrained, sandboxed plugin interface with explicit permissions, resource limits, deterministic inputs and outputs, pinned dependencies and security review. Do not execute arbitrary LLM-generated code directly against patient data.

7. Terminology and semantic contracts

Marketplace matching must compare meaning, not strings. The standard therefore needs stable identifiers for artefacts and fields, code-system references for clinical concepts, explicit units and value sets, and documented mappings between terminology systems.

  • Use canonical identifiers that remain stable across display-name changes.
  • Bind fields to code systems and value sets with versions where available.
  • Declare units and conversion rules; never assume two numeric values are comparable because their labels match.
  • Distinguish absent, unknown, not collected, not applicable and explicitly negative values.
  • Preserve event time, effective time and recorded time when they have different meanings.
  • Represent source and certainty: measured, patient-reported, clinician-entered, imported, inferred or derived.
  • Define mapping confidence and provenance; lossy mappings must be visible.

Terminology services should be a shared platform capability. The authoring tool, marketplace and runtime should use the same terminology resolution and mapping rules so that a package cannot pass authoring checks but fail later due to different interpretation.

8.1 Canonicalization and loss reporting

FHIR Mode must identify the FHIR version, profiles, implementation guide dependencies and SDC features used. The adapter should transform supported FHIR artefacts into the canonical model and produce a mapping report. Unsupported or lossy constructs must be reported, not silently discarded. A FHIR resource being syntactically valid does not automatically mean it is a valid CCIS analytics package or executable pathway.

8.2 Native-first without being closed

Native Standard Mode should be the simplest route for building the full application and marketplace. FHIR Mode should be a first-class interoperability route, not a second-class afterthought. The native model should remain exportable and mappable where possible, and the project should publish implementation guides and examples so external developers can implement the contract.

9. LLM, GUI and code as three equivalent authoring methods

Authors should be free to work in the way that suits them, but all routes must generate the same underlying artefact and pass through the same conformance pipeline.

LLM authoring

Prompt-driven creation, refinement, explanation and test generation. The model produces structured drafts against the specification, asks for missing clinical details and flags uncertainty. It cannot self-approve clinical validity.

GUI authoring

Visual editors for fields, terminology, constraints, pathways, analytic graphs, dependencies and evidence. The GUI writes the same canonical package structure and exposes validation feedback.

Code authoring

SDK, schema definitions, typed interfaces, examples, test harness and command-line validator for developers who prefer code and automated workflows.

Interchange

Authors can move between routes: open a GUI-created package in code, ask an LLM to explain or modify it, and revalidate the resulting version.

9.1 LLM authoring guardrails

  1. Start from the correct artefact template and standard version.
  2. Resolve clinical concepts and terminology rather than inventing codes.
  3. Ask for essential missing information; mark unresolved assumptions explicitly.
  4. Generate a draft, example instances and tests—not a claim of clinical validation.
  5. Run structural and semantic validation and present all errors and warnings.
  6. Show a human-readable diff before changing a reviewed or published package.
  7. Require accountable human review and approval for publication and clinical claims.

The LLM should be a standards-constrained authoring assistant, not an independent authority. The standard provides the boundaries within which it can create and modify packages.

10. Validation, compilation and conformance

Every authoring route should submit to one automated pipeline. The pipeline should produce a machine-readable report and a human-readable explanation, including the exact standard version and validators used.

10.1 Distinct assurance dimensions

DimensionQuestion answeredTypical evidence

Semantic conformanceAre terminology, units, constraints and relationships coherent?Terminology checks, unit tests, mapping checks and semantic consistency rules.
Clinical review / validationIs the clinical content appropriate for its stated intended use and scope?Named reviewers, evidence review, population and setting, review date and limitations.

10.2 Compile to a canonical runtime representation

After validation, the compiler resolves dependencies, pins versions, normalizes supported input formats and emits an immutable runtime manifest. The engine should execute that manifest, not an untrusted authoring document or a mutable branch reference. Compilation should fail closed when mandatory dependencies, features or semantics cannot be resolved.

11. Marketplace discovery and compatibility

The marketplace should index the machine-readable contracts. When a user selects one element, several elements or a pathway, the compatibility resolver compares the selected context with analytic requirements and returns a transparent result.

11.1 Compatibility dimensions

  • Artefact compatibility: required contextual element, group or pathway identifiers and accepted version ranges.
  • Semantic compatibility: required concepts, terminology bindings, units and mappings.
  • Schema compatibility: fields, types, cardinality, constraints and data-model versions.
  • Data readiness: whether the current imported patient data contain sufficient values and time coverage.
  • Runtime compatibility: supported engine version, operators, execution profile and dependencies.
  • Assurance suitability: lifecycle status and validation scope appropriate to the intended use.

11.3 Marketplace search sequence

  1. Read the selected element/group/pathway manifests.
  2. Retrieve candidate analytics by declared context identifiers and semantic tags.
  3. Resolve exact package versions and dependency constraints.
  4. Evaluate schema, terminology, data-readiness and runtime compatibility.
  5. Filter by lifecycle status and intended-use scope.
  6. Present ranked results with explicit match explanations and limitations.
  7. Allow the user to fetch and pin one or multiple packages.

12. Review, lifecycle and version governance

Governance should be visible in the package manifest and backed by an auditable record. Suggested lifecycle states are Draft, Submitted, In Review, Reviewed, Validated, Conditionally Validated, Deprecated, Withdrawn and Rejected. The state must say what was assessed, by whom, against which standard and runtime versions, for which population and intended use, and with what evidence.

“Reviewed” should mean a defined review took place; it should not be presented as proof of clinical effectiveness. “Validated” must always have a scope: for example, structural conformance, execution test suite, retrospective cohort, population, care setting or specific engine release. Conditional validation must display its conditions prominently.

12.1 Separate package version from validation record

Package content is immutable once published. A change creates a new version. Validation records attach to a precise package version and declared scope; a new version does not automatically inherit the previous version's validation. Revalidation can reuse evidence only when the governance policy explicitly permits it and records the rationale.

12.2 Review workflow

  1. Author submits a versioned draft and declares intended use.
  2. Automated structural, semantic, security and test checks run.
  3. Reviewers inspect clinical content, evidence, limitations and test reports.
  4. Required changes are recorded as findings; authors submit a new version.
  5. Authorized maintainers issue a scoped review/validation record.
  6. Marketplace indexes the exact version and status.
  7. Later corrections, deprecation or withdrawal remain visible to users of pinned versions.

13. Runtime execution and provenance

The engine should consume only packages that have passed the required conformance and eligibility checks. It should resolve the package's pinned dependencies, validate the imported patient data, assemble the required context, execute the declared operators and validate the output against its output contract.

13.1 Runtime sequence

  1. Import the EMR Enhancer export or supported FHIR data.
  2. Normalize it into the canonical patient-context representation while preserving source and time semantics.
  3. Resolve selected contextual elements and pathway versions.
  4. Find and fetch compatible analytic package versions from the marketplace.
  5. Check lifecycle, validation scope, runtime support and patient-level data readiness.
  6. Build an execution plan and show missing data, assumptions and parameters.
  7. Run the deterministic core in a controlled environment.
  8. Validate outputs and attach provenance, warnings and limitations.
  9. Present results without obscuring the underlying definitions or source data.

13.2 Execution record

Each run should record the analytic package ID and version, standard version, engine version, operator versions, selected context versions, input dataset reference or governed fingerprint, parameter values, execution time, output schema version, validation messages and result provenance. Sensitive patient data should not be sent to a public GitHub marketplace; the marketplace distributes knowledge packages, while patient data remain in the user's controlled application environment.

LLMs may assist with authoring, explanation, mapping suggestions or narrative summaries, but the core analytic calculation should remain deterministic and reproducible wherever possible. AI-generated interpretations must be labelled, bounded by source results and subject to appropriate review.

14. Evolution and migration

The standard itself needs versioning and governance. Use semantic versioning or an equally explicit compatibility policy for specification releases, with clear distinction between additive changes, breaking changes and deprecated features. Every package declares the standard version it targets.

  • Maintain a published change log and migration guides.
  • Define compatibility windows for supported standard and engine versions.
  • Provide automated migration suggestions, but never silently rewrite published packages.
  • Keep old package versions addressable for reproducibility and audit.
  • Define extension namespaces and a process for promoting widely used extensions into the core.
  • Publish conformance fixtures and reference implementations for each release.

FHIR versions, profiles and implementation guides must be pinned independently from the native standard version. A package may therefore declare both its CCIS version and its FHIR dependencies. Compatibility resolution must consider both.

15. Worked end-to-end example

Consider a contextual element set for diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD), with a pathway and analytics for longitudinal monitoring. The example is illustrative; it is not a clinical guideline or a validated care protocol.

  1. Create the contextual element: an author defines renal function observations, glycaemic measures, medication exposure and relevant event dates. Each field has stable identifiers, terminology, units, time semantics and missingness rules.
  2. Author the element in any route: an LLM draft, GUI-built element or code-authored package produces the same CCIS artefact. In FHIR Mode, the capture component may be represented using FHIR Questionnaire/SDC and mapped to the canonical fields.
  3. Compose a pathway: the pathway references the diabetes and CKD elements, declares stages and decision nodes, and lists the context required at each stage.
  4. Create analytics: one analytic summarizes longitudinal renal function; another evaluates completeness and trends in glycaemic observations. Each declares required fields, accepted units, time windows, operators and outputs.
  5. Validate: automated checks test schema, terminology, units and sample data. Reviewers assess clinical meaning and evidence. Execution tests compare results with expected fixtures. Each assurance result is separately recorded.
  6. Publish: the marketplace indexes immutable versions of the elements, pathway and analytics, with their lifecycle and validation scopes.
  7. Import a patient record: the user imports an EMR Enhancer export. The app normalizes it and preserves source, dates and uncertainty.
  8. Discover compatible analytics: selecting the element set or pathway returns both matching analytics, with data-readiness status and explanations.
  9. Run and inspect: the user fetches and runs one or both. The engine records package and engine versions, inputs, parameters, outputs and provenance. Missing data are reported rather than silently imputed.

This single vertical slice tests the whole standard: authoring equivalence, semantic identity, package dependencies, marketplace matching, FHIR mapping, conformance, runtime execution and auditability.

Chapter 8 · Reusable analytics and research translation

Reusable Clinical Analytics: Templates, Decision Nodes and Research-to-Analytics

From frequently repeated clinical calculations to decision-centred analytics and LLM-assisted translation of research papers into governed, patient-context-aware packages.

A clinical intelligence platform should not require every clinician to design every analysis from scratch. Many clinical questions recur: calculate a score, identify a risk state, assess eligibility, monitor a trajectory, compare a patient with a reference population or determine whether a decision threshold has been crossed. These recurring needs should become reusable analytics templates. More complex analytics should be organised around the clinical decision they support, while research papers should become a source for carefully specified, reviewable analytics—not opaque prompts that directly make clinical decisions.

1. Why reusable analytics matter

Clinical teams repeatedly ask a relatively small set of analytical questions, even when the patients, diagnoses and workflows differ. Repeatedly rebuilding the same logic creates inconsistency, wastes time and makes it difficult to compare results across clinicians or sites. A reusable analytics package captures a defined question, the data it needs, the method it applies, the result it produces, the clinical context in which it is relevant and the limits on its interpretation.

Reusability does not mean that one formula is universally appropriate. A package must declare its intended population, setting, input definitions, units, time windows, missing-data behaviour, version and evidence basis. It should be reusable only where its declared assumptions are satisfied. Compatibility is therefore a clinical and semantic question, not simply a match between two labels.

Core design rule

Build the analytics library from small, testable and versioned units. Make those units composable, but never allow composition to hide a changed definition, an unmet precondition or a clinically important limitation.

2. A library of common analytics templates

The first layer should be a collection of reusable templates for frequent analytical patterns. A template is a parameterised starting point, not automatically a clinically validated decision rule. It becomes a runnable package only after its variables, terminology, formula, intended use, tests and review status are specified.

Parameter changes must create a new configuration or package version and be recorded; they must not silently mutate a reviewed release.

3. Decision-node analytics and the Avinash principle

This book uses the term Avinash principle for a design idea raised in the project: organise analytics around key clinical decision nodes rather than treating analytics as disconnected calculations. The phrase is a project-specific label here, not a claim that a formally published external doctrine has been established. Its operational meaning should be confirmed and refined as the project specification matures.

A decision node is a point in a clinical pathway where the available patient context is assessed to determine what state applies, what uncertainty remains and which next action or branch is appropriate. The analytic attached to that node answers a decision-relevant question. It may use one formula, a set of rules, several upstream analytics or a time-dependent assessment.

For example, a pathway node might ask whether a patient meets a defined escalation criterion. The node can depend on a score, recent trajectory, a contraindication check and data-quality requirements. The node then returns a structured state such as criterion met, criterion not met or cannot determine, with the evidence and rule trace. The node does not need to invent a treatment plan; it supplies a transparent input to the clinician's decision and to the next pathway step.

3.1 A decision node as a contract

A decision-node package should declare:

  • Clinical question: the precise question the node is intended to answer.
  • Context and trigger: the pathway, clinical state or event that makes the node relevant.
  • Inputs: required patient variables, terminology codes, provenance, units and valid time windows.
  • Preconditions: population, setting, data-quality and other eligibility requirements.
  • Logic: deterministic calculations, rule expressions, model invocation or a composition of named analytics.
  • Outcomes: explicit branches, including indeterminate, missing-data and exception states.
  • Action linkage: suggested next pathway node or workflow destination, clearly separated from autonomous clinical action.
  • Explanation: the values and rules that led to the result, plus evidence and limitations.

3.2 Decision graph, not a single score

Clinical decisions often depend on several linked questions. Represent them as a directed graph of decision nodes and analytic dependencies. Each node has a stable identifier and version; edges declare the conditions under which the next node is relevant. The graph should support branches, convergence, loops with explicit stopping conditions and time-based reassessment. Cycles must be bounded and tested to prevent endless execution.

The benefit is traceability: the clinician can see not just a final label, but which decision nodes were evaluated, which were skipped, what evidence each used and why a branch was selected. Analytics can be reused by more than one pathway, while the pathway retains control of sequencing and clinical context.

4. Composing templates into contextual analytics

Reusable templates are the building blocks; contextual analytics are the configured and connected solutions for a particular element, element group or pathway. A contextual analytics package can combine score calculation, trend evaluation, eligibility logic, cohort comparison and a decision graph. The package must declare its dependencies and the exact versions it expects.

Compatibility should be resolved across several dimensions: clinical concept, patient population, setting, terminology, units, required observations, time window, data provenance, pathway stage and package version. If an analytics package expects a specific unit or coding system, a superficial field-name match is insufficient. The resolver should explain both successful matches and incompatibilities.

Composition should preserve each component's provenance and result. A composite result must be inspectable back to the source analytics, inputs, transformations, rule versions and timestamps. If one component is unavailable or invalid, the composite must follow a declared failure policy rather than silently treating missing data as normal.

5. Turning a research paper into an analytics package

A powerful authoring workflow is to provide an LLM with a research paper—such as a PDF, guideline paper, validation study or methods article—and ask it to prepare a contextual analytics package that can later be run against the user's imported patient data. This is not merely “summarise the paper”. It is a structured translation from reported methods and evidence into an executable, testable and reviewable artefact.

5.1 What the LLM should extract

  • Bibliographic identity, publication date, version, study type and source document.
  • Clinical question, intended use, target condition and care setting.
  • Population definition, eligibility criteria, inclusion and exclusion criteria.
  • Index event or index date, baseline period, follow-up period and measurement windows.
  • Variables, operational definitions, units, coding systems, timing and data sources.
  • Formulae, model coefficients, thresholds, decision rules, endpoint definitions and statistical methods.
  • Reported performance, validation population, uncertainty, subgroup findings and limitations.
  • Any assumptions required to translate the published method into the available patient-data model.

5.2 What it should generate

  1. A concise evidence and methods summary with page/table/figure references to the source paper.
  2. A structured analytics specification conforming to the CCIS analytics contract.
  3. Input bindings mapping paper variables to canonical clinical context fields and terminology concepts.
  4. Executable deterministic logic where the paper specifies a reproducible formula or rule.
  5. Optional cohort definition with inclusion/exclusion criteria, index date and observation windows.
  6. Unit, boundary, missing-data and edge-case tests, plus test fixtures based on synthetic examples.
  7. A clinician-facing explanation, intended-use statement, evidence citation and known limitations.
  8. A validation checklist identifying what requires expert interpretation, local adaptation or independent verification.
Critical boundary

The LLM may draft and assemble the package, but it must not fabricate missing coefficients, thresholds, variable definitions or clinical recommendations. Every extracted claim should be traceable to the paper. Ambiguities should become explicit questions or unresolved fields—not guessed values. A paper-derived package is a candidate artefact until reviewed and validated for its intended population and setting.

6. Patient cohort rules: inclusion, exclusion and time windows

The paper-to-analytics workflow should optionally produce a cohort specification alongside the analytic. The user may want to run an analysis on all imported patients or on a selected cohort. Cohort rules define which records qualify for the analysis; they are distinct from the analytic formula itself, even though both are packaged and versioned together.

6.1 Cohort rule components

  • Target population: clinical setting, age range, diagnosis or phenotype, and other defining attributes.
  • Inclusion criteria: conditions that must be satisfied, with explicit operators and time anchors.
  • Exclusion criteria: conditions that disqualify a record, including competing diagnoses, contraindications or prior events where specified.
  • Index date: the event or date from which baseline and follow-up windows are calculated.
  • Look-back and washout: historical periods needed to establish prior status or exclude recent exposure.
  • Observation window: the period during which measurements or outcomes are counted.
  • Data sufficiency: minimum evidence required to classify a record as eligible.
  • Missing and conflicting data: whether the result is excluded, indeterminate or retained with a warning.
  • Deduplication and unit of analysis: patient, encounter, episode or event, with rules for repeated observations.

6.2 Three-valued eligibility

Eligibility should not be reduced to a careless binary result. At minimum, the cohort evaluator should distinguish included, excluded and indeterminate. An indeterminate record may lack a required variable, have conflicting dates or contain insufficient observation time. The package should report criterion-level reasons and counts, so the user can understand how the cohort was formed.

For every cohort run, retain the cohort definition version, source-data snapshot or import identifier, index-date rule, execution time, criterion outcomes and aggregate flow counts. Patient-level results remain within the authorised clinical environment; the marketplace distributes cohort logic, not identifiable patient records.

7. The LLM-assisted authoring workflow

The authoring experience should be a guided pipeline, not a single prompt that jumps from a PDF to a live clinical result:

  1. Upload and identify: accept the research paper and record its bibliographic metadata, document version and provenance.
  2. Extract: identify the clinical question, population, methods, variables, thresholds, endpoints and limitations with source references.
  3. Clarify: ask the author to resolve ambiguities and choose the intended population, setting and local use.
  4. Draft: create the analytics specification, optional cohort definition, terminology bindings and decision-node placement.
  5. Map: map required variables to the canonical context model; show unmapped, ambiguous, unit-incompatible and unavailable data.
  6. Generate: compile the specification into constrained executable logic and generate tests and synthetic fixtures.
  7. Verify: run schema, semantic, unit, boundary, regression and reproducibility checks.
  8. Review: present the paper, extracted evidence, logic, mappings, test results and unresolved assumptions to clinical and technical reviewers.
  9. Publish: release a versioned candidate or reviewed package with an explicit validation scope and lifecycle status.
  10. Run: select compatible patient data or a cohort in the separate Clinical Intelligence App, inspect eligibility and execute the pinned package.

The LLM can assist at every stage, including proposing mappings and explaining failures. It should not bypass deterministic validation, reviewer approval or execution permissions. Generated code should run in a constrained environment with declared inputs, resource limits and no uncontrolled access to patient records or external services.

9. Running analytics on imported patient data

The separate Clinical Intelligence App imports patient-context data produced by EMR Enhancer, assembles the canonical context and resolves compatible analytics. The user can choose a contextual element, a group of elements or a pathway and then select one or more eligible analytics packages. Where a package defines a cohort, the app can apply its cohort rules to the data currently available in the app and show the eligibility flow before running the analytic.

Execution should be reproducible and inspectable. The run record should pin the analytics package version, dependencies, cohort definition, context snapshot, terminology versions, engine version, run time and result status. The result view should show the patient or cohort scope, inputs used, missing inputs, criterion outcomes, decision-node trace, outputs and evidence links. The user should be able to distinguish a result for one patient from an aggregate cohort result.

Marketplace packages contain reusable knowledge and executable definitions, not patient data. Patient context, cohort membership and identifiable run outputs remain in the authorised application boundary. Any export of aggregate results must be separately governed and checked for privacy risk.

10. Clinical validation, uncertainty and safe use

Research translation is vulnerable to subtle errors: a table may define a threshold differently from the abstract; a model may require a variable that is not routinely collected; a validation cohort may differ from the local population; a unit conversion may change a boundary; or a paper may report association rather than a decision rule. The workflow must surface these issues.

Validation should be layered: fidelity to the source method; structural and semantic conformance; software verification; test performance on known and synthetic cases; clinical review; and, where required, local clinical validation and regulatory assessment. Each validation statement must specify the package version, population, setting, data sources and intended use. Revalidation is needed when these change materially.

Outputs should include uncertainty and limitations where the underlying method supports them. Avoid false precision, uncalibrated risk estimates and unsupported recommendations. A decision node should be able to return “insufficient data” or “outside validated scope”. Clinicians remain responsible for interpreting results in the full clinical context; the system should support, not obscure, that judgement.

11. Implementation sequence and the future cohort exporter

The first implementation should not wait for a full cohort-export feature in EMR Enhancer. The two products have distinct responsibilities:

  • EMR Enhancer: creates and exports structured contextual patient data. A dedicated cohort exporter—with cohort selection, inclusion/exclusion builder and batch export—is a planned later capability, not an assumed current feature.
  • Clinical Intelligence App: imports the available contextual data, evaluates package compatibility, applies supported cohort rules within its authorised data boundary, executes analytics and presents results.
  • Marketplace: distributes versioned contextual elements, pathways, templates, analytics packages, evidence metadata and tests—not patient datasets.

A practical sequence is: first implement individual-patient analytics templates on the existing import contract; next support a cohort definition and cohort evaluation inside the Clinical Intelligence App for the data it can already access; then add multi-patient execution and aggregate reporting where the import format and privacy controls permit; finally build the EMR Enhancer cohort exporter and formalise the batch hand-off. This avoids making the analytics engine dependent on a future exporter while preserving a clean contract for it.

12. How this chapter extends the architecture

Chapter 8 adds a reusable analytics layer and a research-to-package authoring workflow to the architecture. Common templates reduce repeated work; decision nodes organise analytics around clinically meaningful questions; pathways compose those nodes into workflows; and research papers can seed new packages through a traceable LLM-assisted process. Optional cohort rules define the population on which a package is run.

The key boundary remains intact: EMR Enhancer produces contextual patient data; the Clinical Intelligence App imports and analyses it; CCIS defines the shared artefact contract; and the marketplace distributes reusable knowledge and analytics. Cohort export from EMR Enhancer is a later extension, not a prerequisite for beginning the analytics engine.

Chapter 8 conclusion

The platform should make it easy to reuse a known analytic, compose analytics around a decision node, or create a new evidence-derived package from a research paper—while ensuring every package is explicit, testable, traceable, compatible and appropriately reviewed before clinical use.

1. The engineering objective

CCIS is currently a proposed project-specific standard. It becomes useful as a standard only when independent authoring tools and runtimes can create, validate, exchange and execute artefacts against the same published rules. Prose alone cannot guarantee this. The immediate objective is to establish a small normative core and a reference implementation that demonstrates conformance.

The first release should cover only the artefacts required for the initial product workflow: a contextual element, a pathway with decision nodes, an analytics package, terminology and data bindings, compatibility declarations, execution inputs and outputs, evidence/provenance and lifecycle metadata. Advanced extensions can follow after the core is stable.

Engineering principle

Specify the smallest complete contract that can support a real workflow. Validate it automatically. Demonstrate it end to end. Expand only when a concrete use case exposes a missing capability.

2. Freeze the first normative specification

Before coding schemas, distinguish normative requirements from explanatory guidance. Normative text states what a conformant artefact or implementation must do. Guidance explains recommended patterns, examples and design rationale. Each requirement should have a stable identifier so tests and implementation reports can refer to it.

The first specification should define:

  • CCIS identity, versioning, namespaces and compatibility policy.
  • Required and optional fields for contextual elements, pathways, decision nodes and analytics packages.
  • Canonical types, identifiers, cardinality, units, terminology references and temporal semantics.
  • Input/output contracts, preconditions, outcomes, failure states and dependency declarations.
  • Provenance, evidence references, authorship, review, validation and lifecycle states.
  • Security and privacy constraints, including the boundary between marketplace artefacts and patient data.
  • FHIR Mode mapping rules and explicit handling of unsupported or lossy transformations.

Use a requirements matrix: requirement ID, normative statement, artefact affected, validation method, test case and implementation status. Avoid declaring a feature “supported” merely because a field exists in a schema; specify the behaviour a conformant implementation must provide.

3. Build machine-readable schemas

Choose a machine-readable representation for the first release—such as JSON with JSON Schema—and publish the schema version alongside the human-readable specification. The exact technology is less important than deterministic validation, clear versioning and broad tooling support.

Keep schemas strict enough to reject unknown or malformed critical fields, while providing a deliberate extension mechanism for future additions. Define how older readers handle newer optional fields and how breaking changes require a new major version. Never use an unversioned schema that changes meaning in place.

4. Build the conformance toolchain

A validator should be a reusable service or library used by the LLM authoring workflow, GUI builder, code authoring tools, marketplace ingestion and runtime preflight. All authoring modes must converge on the same artefact representation and the same validation pipeline.

Validation should occur in layers:

  1. Syntax: the document parses and has the expected encoding and format.
  2. Structural: the artefact conforms to the correct schema and version.
  3. Reference integrity: all referenced elements, nodes, terminology sets and dependencies resolve.
  4. Semantic: units, value types, time semantics, terminology and clinical concept bindings are coherent.
  5. Graph and compatibility: pathways are traversable; analytics requirements are compatible with declared contexts.
  6. Executable safety: logic uses permitted operations, declared inputs and constrained execution resources.
  7. Test verification: test fixtures produce expected outputs and edge cases are covered.
  8. Governance: review and validation claims match the exact artefact version and scope.

Return machine-readable diagnostics with severity, stable rule ID, JSON path or artefact location, message and remediation hint. A useful validator explains why an artefact failed and how to fix it; it should not merely return “invalid”. Preserve validation reports as part of the package's audit history.

5. Publish reference artefacts and fixtures

The standard needs canonical examples that are small enough to understand and complete enough to test. Build a reference set containing:

  • One simple contextual element with a few typed fields, terminology bindings and provenance.
  • One pathway containing multiple elements, at least two decision nodes and an explicit indeterminate branch.
  • One reusable analytics template, such as a transparent score or threshold calculation.
  • One research-derived analytics candidate with evidence traceability, cohort rules and test fixtures.
  • One FHIR Mode example with a mapping report and declared limitations.
  • One invalid example for each major validator layer, with expected diagnostics.

Provide synthetic patient fixtures only. Include normal, boundary, missing, conflicting, out-of-scope and unit-conversion cases. Reference outputs should be independently calculated and reviewed. These examples become the conformance test suite for future authoring tools and runtimes.

6. Implement the smallest end-to-end runtime

The first vertical slice should prove the entire contract with one clinical use case, not attempt to support every clinical domain. A practical slice is:

  1. Create or select one CCIS contextual element and one short pathway.
  2. Author one analytics package using the LLM, GUI or code; ensure all routes produce the same schema-valid artefact.
  3. Run the shared conformance validator and resolve all blocking diagnostics.
  4. Publish the artefacts to a local or GitHub-backed test marketplace with versioned manifests.
  5. Import one synthetic contextual patient record through the current EMR Enhancer export contract.
  6. Assemble the canonical context and resolve the compatible analytics package.
  7. Execute deterministic logic in a constrained runtime and validate the result schema.
  8. Display the result, input trace, decision-node path, evidence and provenance.
  9. Store an immutable run record identifying all artefact and engine versions.

This slice proves the core architecture: authoring, conformance, publication, import, compatibility, execution and audit. It should be possible to reproduce the same output from the same pinned inputs and versions. Only after this works should the team expand to multi-analytics composition, cohort execution, advanced decision graphs and larger marketplace discovery.

8. Conformance, safety and acceptance tests

Define acceptance criteria before implementation. At minimum:

  • Valid examples pass; malformed or unsupported artefacts fail with actionable diagnostics.
  • LLM-, GUI- and code-authored versions of the same artefact pass the same validator and have equivalent semantics.
  • All pathway references resolve; decision graphs have no unintended dead ends or unbounded cycles.
  • Unit mismatches, missing required inputs and unsupported terminology mappings are surfaced.
  • Inclusion and exclusion criteria produce expected included, excluded and indeterminate cohort outcomes.
  • Decision nodes return explicit branches and preserve the rule trace.
  • Repeated runs with identical pinned inputs, versions and engine configuration are reproducible where deterministic execution is claimed.
  • Package execution cannot access undeclared network, filesystem or patient-data resources.
  • Marketplace publication contains no patient records or cohort membership data.
  • Every clinical validation claim names the exact version, population, setting and intended use.

Clinical safety review must remain distinct from software conformance. Passing the CCIS validator means that an artefact meets defined structural and technical contracts; it does not prove that the clinical method is correct, appropriate or safe for every setting.

10. Definition of done

The first engineering milestone is complete when a developer can take a published example package, validate it, import a synthetic contextual patient record, resolve compatibility, run the analytics and inspect a reproducible result with full provenance—without manual edits to the package or hidden assumptions in the runtime.

The standard milestone is complete when the normative specification, schemas, validator, reference examples, test suite, versioning policy and at least one conformant implementation are published together. Conformance claims should identify the exact CCIS release and test-suite version. A self-declared “CCIS compatible” label without this evidence should not be accepted.

Once the first conformant vertical slice is working, the next work should be driven by implementation evidence. Scaling the Clinical Intelligence Platform: Cohorts, Decision Graphs and Interoperability

Turning the first working CCIS implementation into a dependable platform for reusable analytics, patient-level and cohort-level execution, clinical decision graphs, FHIR exchange and governed community contributions.

1. From vertical slice to platform

A vertical slice proves that the contracts connect: a conformant package can be validated, matched to imported context, executed and audited. A platform must do this repeatedly across different clinical domains, data shapes, patient timelines, package versions and user roles. The transition therefore has four dimensions: breadth of supported knowledge, depth of execution, interoperability and trustworthy governance.

Do not confuse breadth with maturity. Ten loosely specified analytics are less valuable than a smaller catalogue whose input requirements, units, missing-data behaviour, evidence, intended use and tests are explicit. Expansion should preserve the original architecture: EMR Enhancer creates and exports patient context; the separate Clinical Intelligence App imports it and runs marketplace knowledge packages; the marketplace stores reusable knowledge, never patient records.

Expansion rule

Every new capability must extend the same canonical context model, CCIS artefact contracts, conformance pipeline and provenance model. Avoid parallel formats or feature-specific execution paths.

2. Grow the reusable analytics catalogue

Start with a deliberately curated catalogue of common analytics templates. Prioritise repeated clinical needs that can be expressed with clear inputs and outputs: threshold interpretation, trend detection, score calculation, staging support, medication-safety checks, monitoring gaps, eligibility checks and follow-up reminders. Templates should be generic building blocks, not hard-coded disease-specific screens.

Each catalogue item should declare its purpose, intended population, required and optional inputs, terminology bindings, units, time semantics, missingness rules, output schema, evidence references, contraindications, version and test fixtures. A template is not clinically reusable merely because its code can be called from several places; it is reusable when its meaning and operating assumptions travel with it.

Keep three layers distinct:

  • Template: a reusable analytical pattern, such as a trend, threshold, score or eligibility rule.
  • Clinical package: a governed configuration of one or more templates for a defined contextual element, pathway or use case.
  • Execution instance: a run of a particular package version against a particular patient context or cohort, with recorded inputs and outputs.

Catalogue discovery should filter by clinical concept, decision node, pathway, population, required data, intended setting, evidence status and compatibility. Search results must distinguish draft, reviewed, validated and deprecated packages. A package should never become “validated” solely because its schema is valid.

3. Make decision graphs authorable and executable

The Avinash principle positions analytics around clinically meaningful decision nodes: a node represents a finding, observation, state or question that can change what the clinician should consider next. The next engineering step is to make these graphs explicit CCIS artefacts rather than implicit branching buried in application code.

A decision graph needs typed nodes, typed edges, entry conditions, branch predicates, terminal states, required data, missing-data routes, evidence links and explanatory output. Each branch should be inspectable. The graph should record why a path was taken and which data values or rules caused that transition.

Provide three authoring surfaces over the same underlying representation:

  • Visual builder: clinicians and informaticians arrange nodes and branches, configure conditions and inspect the graph.
  • Structured/code editor: developers review exact expressions, schemas, identifiers and version-controlled diffs.
  • LLM-assisted authoring: a model proposes nodes, conditions, mappings and explanations from a clinical specification or research source; it cannot publish or clinically validate its own output.

All three routes must compile to the same canonical graph, pass the same static checks and execute through the same deterministic runtime. A graph must define what happens when an input is absent, stale, conflicting, outside the supported range or not interpretable. “Unknown” must not silently become “false” or “normal”.

4. Build the cohort execution engine

Cohort execution extends analytics from one patient's context to a defined set of patients. Treat cohort definition and analytics execution as separate but linked artefacts. A cohort specification selects records using explicit population rules; an analytics package defines what is calculated for each eligible patient or across the cohort.

The cohort contract should represent:

  • Inclusion and exclusion criteria with typed variables and explicit Boolean logic.
  • Index event or index date, look-back period, washout period, follow-up window and censoring rules.
  • Age and other eligibility boundaries, where appropriate, with units and temporal reference.
  • Handling of repeated events, duplicate records, transfers, incomplete histories and conflicting observations.
  • Three-valued evaluation: eligible, ineligible and indeterminate when the available data cannot establish the rule.
  • Per-patient eligibility explanations and a reproducible cohort summary.

Execution should occur in stages: validate the cohort definition; resolve data requirements; evaluate eligibility; produce a cohort manifest; execute the selected analytics for eligible records; aggregate outputs only where the package defines valid aggregation; and produce a provenance report. Retain the reason each record was included, excluded or marked indeterminate. The system must not conceal data-quality limitations behind a single cohort count.

For multi-patient work, apply access controls, purpose limitation, minimum-necessary data, audit logging and appropriate de-identification or aggregation controls. The cohort engine should not assume that every user who can run an individual-patient analytic is authorised to run population-level analysis.

5. Add the future EMR Enhancer cohort exporter

The cohort engine can be developed and tested before the EMR Enhancer cohort exporter exists. Initially, use synthetic datasets and explicitly supported imported data bundles to prove cohort rules, indeterminate handling, patient-level execution and aggregate reporting. This avoids coupling progress in the Clinical Intelligence App to a future feature in the EMR product.

When the exporter is built later, it should emit a versioned, documented cohort data package that follows the same canonical context and CCIS contracts. It should include the cohort definition or reference, export timestamp, data coverage, source system, patient and record identifiers appropriate to the authorised workflow, terminology and unit information, temporal boundaries and a manifest of included records. It must not export more data than the declared purpose requires.

Keep the boundary clear: EMR Enhancer is responsible for assembling and exporting the authorised patient/cohort context; the Clinical Intelligence App is responsible for importing, normalising, evaluating cohort rules and executing analytics. The marketplace remains a repository of reusable knowledge packages and must not become a patient-data exchange.

Dependency decision

Build the cohort engine now against test fixtures and supported imports. Build the EMR Enhancer cohort exporter as a later integration milestone, with a contract test proving that its output is accepted by the same import pipeline.

6. Harden FHIR Mode and interoperability

FHIR Mode should be a deliberate interoperability profile, not a second internal data model. Define supported FHIR releases, resources, profiles, extensions, terminology bindings and mapping limitations. Where structured data capture is needed, specify how relevant SDC artefacts are imported or exported and how their semantics map to the CCIS canonical model.

For every mapping, document whether it is lossless, conditionally lossless or lossy. Preserve source identifiers and provenance. Do not imply that a FHIR Questionnaire or QuestionnaireResponse alone carries every meaning required by a contextual pathway, decision graph or executable analytics package. Where no direct representation exists, use a documented extension or a linked CCIS artefact rather than silently dropping meaning.

Build round-trip tests where round-tripping is claimed, terminology and unit tests, profile validation, version compatibility tests and negative fixtures for unsupported structures. Publish a clear capability matrix so implementers know what is supported, what is transformed and what remains outside the profile.

7. Establish external review and release governance

A standard and marketplace become more credible when the rules for change are visible and contributors can challenge them. Establish a lightweight governance process before the ecosystem becomes large: maintainers, clinical reviewers, technical reviewers, release manager and a documented route for proposals and issue resolution. Early governance can be small, but decisions must be recorded.

Use a proposal process for new core fields, terminology bindings, graph constructs, execution semantics and breaking changes. Require a rationale, use cases, compatibility analysis, example artefacts, migration plan and tests. Separate editorial corrections from normative changes. Publish release notes, deprecation periods and security advisories.

Review roles should remain distinct: technical conformance review checks the specification; clinical review assesses intended use and clinical logic; evidence review checks source quality and applicability; operational/security review assesses execution and data handling. One approval must not be treated as a substitute for the others.

8. Evaluate clinical quality and operational reliability

Schema validity and deterministic execution do not prove clinical correctness. Each package needs a validation plan appropriate to its risk and intended use. That plan may include expert review, comparison with reference calculations, retrospective test sets, edge-case analysis, subgroup checks, prospective evaluation where warranted and post-release monitoring.

Measure technical reliability separately from clinical performance. Technical measures include validation failures, execution errors, latency, reproducibility, version drift and provenance completeness. Clinical measures may include agreement with an accepted reference, sensitivity or specificity for a defined task, calibration, alert burden, missing-data rates and clinician usability. Choose metrics according to the analytic's purpose; do not claim one generic accuracy score validates every package.

Use synthetic and carefully governed test data during development. For every release, preserve a known test corpus and expected outputs. Record limitations and known failure modes in the package metadata and user-facing result. Provide a safe failure mode: if required inputs or execution conditions are not met, report that the analytic could not be reliably run rather than manufacturing a confident result.

9. Use LLMs as governed authoring assistants

LLMs can accelerate the creation of templates, decision graphs, cohort rules, terminology mappings, test cases and explanatory text. They can also help translate a research paper into a candidate analytics package. Their output should be treated as a draft artefact with traceable source references, not as an approved clinical rule.

The authoring pipeline should require the model to identify the source passage or table supporting each substantive rule, state assumptions, flag missing information, map variables to available data elements and produce executable tests. The validator can detect structural and certain semantic errors; it cannot determine whether a paper's findings are appropriate for a particular patient or local clinical setting.

Require human review before publication, and risk-proportionate clinical validation before use. Keep prompts, model/version metadata, source document identifiers, generated artefact version, reviewer decisions and subsequent edits in the audit trail, subject to privacy and licensing constraints. Do not upload identifiable patient data to a research-paper authoring workflow unless the environment and permissions explicitly support that use.

11. Definition of a mature platform

The platform has reached this stage when clinicians and developers can author or select a governed contextual element, pathway or analytics package; validate and discover compatible packages; run deterministic patient-level analytics; define and execute reproducible cohorts; inspect decision paths and explanations; exchange supported data through a documented FHIR profile; and trace every result to the exact artefact version, input context, execution and review status.

It should also support safe failure, transparent uncertainty, role-appropriate access, privacy-preserving cohort workflows, package deprecation and migration, independent review and measurable quality monitoring. No single LLM, GUI or runtime should be able to bypass these controls.

Chapter 10 conclusion

After proving CCIS with one conformant vertical slice, expand in evidence-led increments: curate reusable analytics, make decision graphs first-class executable artefacts, build cohort execution independently of the future exporter, harden FHIR mappings and establish external governance. This turns the architecture into a sustainable clinical intelligence ecosystem without weakening its shared standard or safety boundaries.
